# Creative Commons licences suit papers, figures, slides and data, and not software

A Creative Commons licence is built from a base and up to three conditions.
The base `BY` asks for attribution in every licence.
The condition `SA`, ShareAlike, asks that an adaptation carry the same licence.
The condition `NC`, NonCommercial, forbids a commercial use.
The condition `ND`, NoDerivatives, forbids sharing an adaptation, such as a translation or a figure redrawn from the original.
`CC0 1.0` is not a licence of this family but a dedication to the public domain, with a permissive licence as a fallback where the law forbids the dedication.
Version 4.0 is the current version of the licences.
The six licences of version 4.0 are `CC BY`, `CC BY-SA`, `CC BY-NC`, `CC BY-NC-SA`, `CC BY-ND` and `CC BY-NC-ND`.
arXiv offers four of them and CC0, as knowledge item `public-knowledge-base/arxiv/licences-and-submission-rules.md` lists them.

A Creative Commons licence is irrevocable.
A user who received the work under the licence may keep using it under its terms after the author stops offering it.

Creative Commons recommends against its licences for software, because they say nothing about source code and patents.
Code goes under a licence of software, as knowledge item `public-knowledge-base/licences/software-licences.md` says.
CC0 is the exception that the Free Software Foundation accepts for code, and it still advises against CC0 for software, because CC0 states that it grants no patent right.
A repository that holds both code and text may license each under its own licence, as knowledge item `public-knowledge-base/licences/applying-a-licence.md` says.

A figure, a table or a passage taken from a work under `CC BY` may enter a paper with a credit line.
The credit names the author, the title, the source and the licence, and says whether the material was changed.
A figure taken from a work under `ND` may enter unchanged, and a redrawn or cropped version needs the permission of the author.
A figure taken from a work without an open licence needs the permission of the copyright holder, apart from what the quotation right of the law allows.
The copyright holder of a published paper is often its publisher, as knowledge item `public-knowledge-base/licences/publishers-and-preprints.md` says.
